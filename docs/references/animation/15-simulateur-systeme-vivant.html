How I Turn Any Industry Into a Live System

Nyxera (@nyxera0x) · Source sur X

My first simulators looked expensive and nobody stopped scrolling. 5 rules fixed that. Claude Code subagents built a whole airport on them. Below: the brief, the checks and every prompt.

My first simulators had my own look. Real proportions, a camera flying between shots like a film, a port in clay orange. They looked expensive and nobody stopped scrolling.

So I threw the look out and wrote down 5 rules. None of them is about the 3D:

  • One camera. One angle for the whole video. It only pans and zooms, the way someone works an app with a mouse. No cuts.
  • Toy scale. A forklift nearly as tall as the dock door. Big simple shapes, soft edges everywhere.
  • 2 colours. Blue and white on a lavender ground. Yellow only on the working machines.
  • Real software on top. A site switcher, live cards, a tracking bar, a fleet table. You believe someone uses it at work.
  • Game-style selection. A thin wireframe box round the vehicle you clicked, a glow under it, its route drawn on the ground.

Then I built an airport on those 5 rules and nothing else.

Every model is code. 2 subagents wrote 3,277 lines: 26 builders, from a 46 m jet to a traffic cone. There's no 3D file and no download.

At the end there's a free Claude Code skill with the airport inside, the brief I gave the subagents and every prompt below.

What You're Building

One HTML page and a 50-second film made from it. 6 parts:

  • The rules. The camera, the scale, the palette, the interface and the selection, pinned down before any code.
  • The brief. One file both subagents read: the frames, the colours, the scale every model shares.
  • The kit. Every model as a function: a jet in 2 liveries, a pier with jet bridges, a cargo terminal, 15 ground vehicles, trees, fences, city blocks.
  • The day. One container's trip from the hold to the rack, and everything around it, written as a function of time.
  • The interface. Matched panel by panel over a frame of the look you want.
  • The film. One camera, 10 clicks, a dropdown that jumps between sites, a score in code.

What You Need

  • Claude Code on any plan that includes it. I ran everything on [top model].
  • Node.js 20 or newer.
  • ffmpeg and Python 3 for the video and the sound. Claude Code installs both if you ask.
  • Optional: an ElevenLabs key for the music. Without it the skill scores the film in code.

Step 1. Install the Skill

npx skills add Nyxera0x/simulator -g -a claude-code

Then, once, in the installed folder:

cd ~/.claude/skills/simulator && npm install && npx playwright install chromium

Open Claude Code and type /simulator.

Step 2. Pin the Look Down

Don't describe the look you want. Measure it.

/simulator Take this video apart: [a video with the look you want].
1. Extract a frame every 3 seconds at full size and make a contact sheet.
2. Write down the camera: does it cut or move continuously, its angle, how it gets from one place to the next.
3. Sample the colours from the frames with ffmpeg: the ground, the roads, the brand colour, the light parts, the accent, the trees.
4. List the interface panel by panel, with each panel's position and size as a share of the frame.
5. List what a selected object gets: outline, glow, label, route.
Give me the rules as a short list. No opinions.

The colours come back as hex codes. The ground I wanted is #d5def7, a pale lavender. My first render turned it into #e9eaed, office-carpet grey, until I changed one line. That's Step 4.

Step 3. Write the Brief and Split the Models

Write a brief for 2 subagents that build the models for [an airport] in that look.
Put in it: the path to the frames, the palette, and the rule "toy proportions, premium finish: generous bevels on every edge, matte paint with a soft sheen, crisp printed logos and numbers, nothing sharp".
Write a scale contract both of them must meet: [the jet's cargo door sills at 3.2 m and its passenger doors at 5.0 m; the loader's deck reaches 3.2, the catering box reaches 5.0].
Agent A: [the jet in 2 liveries, the terminal pier, the jet bridge, the cargo terminal, the tower, a hangar, trees, fences, city blocks].
Agent B: [every ground vehicle]. Start with [one box truck and one forklift], get them right against the frames, then give every other vehicle the same treatment.
Each one renders stills, puts them next to the frames and fixes what doesn't match. Each one exports every model as a function with its moving parts: [setFront(h), setBox(h), setForks(h), roll(distance)].

They ran in parallel: 30 minutes for the vehicles, 35 for the airside, about 800,000 tokens between them. The scale contract held. The high loader's deck stops at the hold's sill, and the catering box stops at the passenger door, without me touching a number.

Step 4. Fix the Light

Both subagents flagged the same problem without being asked. The stage used ACES tone mapping, and ACES pulls pale colours towards grey. The lavender lot came out grey and the blue looked tired.

Switch the renderer to Neutral tone mapping at exposure 0.93, the sun at 2.2, the sky light at 1.0. Render one still and sample the ground. It must land within 10 of [#d5def7] on every channel.

One line of code brought the whole palette back.

Step 5. Match the Interface Over a Frame

Build the interface as HTML over the canvas at 1600x900. Put [one frame of the look you want] under it at 50% opacity and match every panel to it: position, size, corner radius, font size. Use our own words: [Rampline, Pier B, ULDs on hand, Turnaround Tracking].
Panels: [a top bar with a site switcher and its dropdown, 3 KPI cards with solid blue icons, a zoom column, a detail card with a thumbnail of the selected vehicle and 3 buttons, a tracker with icons in its steps, a fleet table with a segmented control].
Render every thumbnail from the models themselves.

The overlay caught a bug no description would have. The progress bar in the detail card kept landing at the bottom of the card. A class name in my CSS clashed with one in the kit, and the kit's version was absolutely positioned.

Step 6. Write the Day as a Function of Time

Write the airport's work as a pure function of time t. One hero: [container AKE 48211 AU]:
- [1.4 s] the high loader lowers it from the hold, [4.4 s] it rolls onto the tug's third dolly
- [6.0 to 13.0 s] the tug tows 3 dollies to Cargo C, every dolly following the tug's path at its own distance behind
- [14.2 s] a forklift drives out of dock D3, lifts it and carries it in
Around it: [a truck backs into D6, a pushback at B11, catering at B10, belt loaders running, a second tug on the service road].
Every vehicle moves along a path by distance, never by physics. Render any second out of order and it must look the same.

The pushback took one idea. The jet follows its path tail first, so its nose points against the direction it moves. The tug keeps 1 m clear of the nose.

Step 7. One Camera, No Cuts

Direct it like a person using the app, not like a film:
- one angle for the whole film: [azimuth 38, elevation 36]. The camera only pans and zooms.
- between sites it glides: out on the way, back in on arrival
- the cursor clicks what the story is about: [a container, a tug, a forklift, a truck]. The detail card switches on the click.
- the selected vehicle gets a thin blue wireframe box, a glow on the ground and its route drawn ahead of it
- to change site, the cursor opens the switcher's dropdown and picks the next one

My earlier films cut between 13 shots. This one never cuts. It reads like software you could open.

Step 8. Check That Nothing Drives Through Anything

Write a collision check. Pose the world every 0.1 s for the whole film. Give every vehicle an oriented box from its own geometry and every jet a box round its fuselage. Report every pair that overlaps, every vehicle inside a tree crown, every truck inside a building. Skip the pairs that are meant to touch: [a container on its dolly, a loader at the door, a jet bridge at its jet]. Fix the paths until it reports nothing.

The first cut looked fine to me. The first person I showed it to saw vehicles drive through each other.

Reading the paths, I found 4: the tug train drove through a parked truck, a second tug through a tree, the pushback tug sat inside the jet's nose, and the forklifts drove through the pallets in the docks. The check found 2 more I'd missed: after the push, the jet taxied straight through its own tug, and a forklift nosed into the dolly. The 7th, a fuel truck with its cab inside a wing, I caught on a close-up still. The check only knows fuselages.

Step 9. Score It and Render It

Score it in code on the same timetable: [the loader's hydraulics, the container rolling over the deck rollers, the tug's electric hum, the forklift's reverse beeps, a truck's reversing beeper, a shutter rolling up, a jet's engines spooling up]. Lay real airport ambience under it from Openverse, CC0 only. Get a music track from ElevenLabs with its sections cut to the film's own beats. Master to -14 LUFS.
Render every frame at 60 fps and 1920x1080 in headless Chrome, H.264. Show me a contact sheet.

The film runs 50 seconds. The cursor clicks 10 times. Not one frame is a cut.

Reuse the kit for [a car plant]. Keep the brief, the light, the interface and the camera. New models: [the plant's buildings and machines], built by 2 subagents against the same frames. New hero: [one car body from the press to the truck].
