[top model] Field Guide: Make Claude Build Games, Videos and 3D Worlds

wluha (@cryptowluha) · Source sur X

Anthropic released [top model] on November 9, a little before 7 pm UTC. Two days later my feed is basically one long demo reel.

I make small animated films with Claude, so I wanted to know one thing: is this a better version of what we already had, or does it change what one person can make in an evening?

So I spent most of those two days reading. Every post with a real demo, every reply where someone asked "how long did it take?" and the author actually answered. Below is what I found, with the numbers people gave themselves.

The short version: the walls between "making a video", "making a game" and "making a 3D model" are getting thin. Several of the best posts are all three at once.

The basics, quickly

These are from Anthropic's own documentation, not from hype posts:

  • it's Anthropic's most capable model that's widely available (Mythos 5.2 is the sibling for verified organizations)
  • 1M tokens of context, up to 128K tokens in one answer
  • $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output, cheaper than [previous top model] was ($10 / $50)
  • for comparison, [advanced model] costs $4 / $20, so [top model] is still twice the price per token
  • thinking is always on, and the docs suggest starting at effort high and only going higher where you can see a difference

That last point matters for the cost numbers below. A lot of these runs were long.

Games

This is where most of the noise is, and honestly most of it is earned.

The post that set the tone came from Felix Rieseberg, who works at Anthropic. The goal was to test [top model] with one-shot prompts and zero human feedback. Everything in the game is made from scratch: the models, the sounds, the textures. Around 2.7M output tokens and 445M input tokens, heavily cached, about $550 at API prices, 8 hours of running. It's playable as a Claude artifact. 3.5 million people have looked at it so far.

$550 sounds like a lot until you remember what a small game studio charges per day.

Matt Shumer went for scale: a "Mirror Earth" you can walk around and destroy, where the weather matches the real planet in real time.

Kevin Ngo, whose code-drawn animations went everywhere at the [advanced model] launch, made a dark fantasy boss fight that runs in the browser. The reply under it says you can't lose, which I appreciate.

The one I learned the most from was Loktar's "Lanternfall". Characters, animations, death effects, all prompted, no outside assets. Someone asked how the animation came out so clean, and the answer was a tip I'm stealing: ask it to use skeletal animation, then have it analyze the walk cycle frame by frame until it decides the motion is right.

That's the same habit I use for my own films. Make it look at single frames before you trust the moving result.

And for the people who want real engines: a full racing game in Unity in three hours, five cars and a track, with the models, textures and effects generated by the model itself.

Matthew Berman, who says they burned through over 100 billion tokens during early testing, called it the most capable model they've used for 3D, especially at controlling Unreal Engine and Blender, and showed a Gears of War clone as proof.

Video

Video is the part closest to what I do, and the posts here are less about the result and more about how little was asked.

Matt Shumer's video got 277K views, and when someone asked for the prompt, the honest answer was that it was something like "Make me a video that will blow me away", started by accident in an unrelated repo. The model took the repo as material and ran with it.

Timur Negru did something I hadn't seen before: one prompt, plus a PDF with notes and photos of a ruin in Tuscany they're thinking of buying and restoring. [top model] turned that into a short film about the place, audio included.

That's a use case I keep thinking about. You hand it your own documents and photos, and it makes a video about your actual life, without any stock footage.

Chetaslua's piece is pure JavaScript, no outside assets. The recipe, straight from their reply: attach a few images in a style you love, describe the story, set the rule "pure js, don't use external assets other than the style references", and let it cook. About 30 minutes.

Kevin Ngo also made a music video with Python, rendered in Blender, the same route as the GIFs from the [advanced model] launch, just with a lot more going on in the frame.

And polinaaAi gave the most precise numbers of anyone: a nature game recorded as video, 1,920 frames at 1080p and 60 fps, 30 minutes of rendering, $37 in tokens. That works out to about $1.16 per second of finished footage.

3D

Flavio's post is three words and a video ("insanely good at 3D"), and the reply says it took five minutes. That's a fair summary of what a lot of people felt on day one.

The most useful 3D post for me was a head-to-head. EnvolDev ran the exact same "Cathedral of Ice" prompt on [top model] and [advanced model]. [top model] cost more, $81.10 against $45.97, but finished in 1 hour 57 minutes instead of 3 hours 11. Look at the ice and the whale and decide for yourself if the difference is worth $35.

I like this post because it doesn't pretend [top model] is free. It's faster, it's prettier, and it costs more. Those can all be true at once.

BlendiByl went somewhere else entirely: a game that looks hand-painted, where every frame is around 1.5 million brush strokes, running at 200 fps in a browser tab. Under an hour, and the code is open source.

Where it stops being software

Anthropic's own launch thread had the example that surprised me most, and it has nothing to do with screens. From a one-paragraph prompt, [top model] designed three working electronic devices: it wrote the specs, picked the components, routed four-layer circuit boards, designed enclosures for 3D printing and wrote the software. Each board was ready for manufacture in under a day, and all three worked without changes.

The 3D-printed enclosures are the bit that connects to everything above. The same model that sculpts a whale in a game can design a case you can hold.

The part where it all merges

Here's what I keep noticing. The best posts don't fit one category.

Felix's game is 3D models, sound design and game code in one run. Polina's game was made to be filmed. Kevin's music video is 3D, animation and music at once. Timur's film started as a PDF about a house.

And then venturetwins asked [top model] to "make a masterpiece". It opened a browser clone of MS Paint and painted a lake at dusk: 11,066 brush strokes, 261 colors each mixed by typing three numbers into a dialog box, 94 minutes. Then it generated a song for the timelapse and wrote an artist statement to go with it.

So "should I use it for video or games or 3D?" is kind of the wrong question now. You describe the thing you want to exist, and the format becomes the last step.

What I'd take from all of this before trying it

These are the patterns that showed up again and again in the replies, plus what Anthropic recommends in its own docs.

  1. Ask for less, show more. The best results came from short prompts plus real material: a PDF and photos, style images, a repo. Anthropic's docs even warn that prompts written for older models tend to be too prescriptive for this one.
  2. Start at effort high. That's the documented starting point. The longest, most expensive runs here were people pushing it on purpose.
  3. Make it check its own frames. Loktar's walk-cycle trick works for any animation: have it look at individual frames until the motion is right.
  4. Set a budget before you press enter. The range above goes from $37 to $550. Long autonomous runs can go for hours.
  5. Know when [advanced model] is enough. At half the price per token, it still did the same Cathedral of Ice scene, just slower. For quick drafts I'd start there and save [top model] for the run that has to look its best.

Before you press enter

Reading the replies under all these demos, the same few habits kept coming up. None of them are complicated, and most of them are about what happens before the model writes a single line.

1. Plan with [top model], build with [advanced model]

Matt Shumer summed up what a lot of people landed on in the first day: [top model] for planning, [advanced model] for execution.

It makes sense with the prices. [top model] is the one you want thinking about the shape of the game or the shot list. The long, repetitive part, writing hundreds of files or rendering frames, can go to [advanced model] at half the price per token. You don't have to do this for every project, but for anything that runs for hours it adds up fast.

2. Turn your idea into a brief before you build

Voxyz shared a prompt trick I've already started using. Don't ask [top model] to "optimize my prompt". Ask it to write a task brief you could hand directly to an engineer. If you ask it to optimize a prompt, it assumes the reader knows nothing and writes a tutorial. If you frame it as a job for someone capable who hasn't seen the project, you get a tight brief that leaves the right decisions open.

The same post suggests giving it your ideas, screenshots and constraints first, and asking it to point out what you haven't thought through before any code gets written. For a game, that's the moment it asks you how the player loses. For a video, how long each shot holds.

3. Use high effort, keep max for when you mean it

Peter Gostev let [top model] run overnight at Max effort on a Roman city. The result is beautiful, and the bill was $2,618.

That's the clearest warning in the whole launch. Anthropic's own docs suggest starting at high and only going higher where you can measure a difference. I'd add one thing: before any long run, tell it how much time or money it has, and ask it to stop and report when it gets close.

4. Feed it your own material

Timur's ruin in Tuscany, chetaslua's style images, the floor plans people are turning into walkthroughs. The best results almost always started from something real the author attached. Everestchris6 showed a nice version of this for 3D: give [top model] a 2D floor plan plus photos of the space, and it builds a 3D walkthrough to scale that you can tour in the browser.

5. Make it check its own work

Loktar's walk-cycle tip from the games section is one example. Felix's whole game was a test of how far it gets with no human feedback at all. In both cases the model was the one looking at its own output and deciding what to fix. Ask for that explicitly: screenshots at key moments, single frames from the animation, a playthrough before it says "done".

Try it yourself

Here are three starter prompts, one for each thing people are making. I built them from the methods in the posts above, so each one carries a trick from someone who already got it working. Swap the parts in brackets for your own idea.

To be upfront: these are my prompts, not copies of the authors' prompts, and I haven't run these exact versions on [top model] yet. They're a starting point that follows what worked for the people above. Results will vary with your idea, your material and the effort level you pick.

What you need first

The game and the 3D walkthrough work right in the Claude app: Claude builds an HTML page and shows it in the preview, and you can download the file and open it in your browser.

Video is different. To get an actual MP4, the model needs to render frames and stitch them together, and that only works where it can run programs on your computer. That means Claude Code, plus two free tools:

bash
# macOS (Terminal)
brew install node ffmpeg
mkdir my-video
cd my-video
npm init -y
npm i -D playwright
npx playwright install chromium

# Windows (PowerShell; open a new window after the two winget lines)
winget install OpenJS.NodeJS.LTS
winget install Gyan.FFmpeg
mkdir my-video
cd my-video
npm init -y
npm i -D playwright
npx playwright install chromium

Playwright opens a browser in the background so Claude can capture each frame, and ffmpeg turns those frames into a video. Install Claude Code itself from Anthropic's docs, then pick [top model] with /model.

If you only have the Claude app, you can still use the video prompt: you'll get an animation as an HTML page, and you can record it with your screen recorder. It won't be frame-perfect, but it's fine for a first try.

A game

Built on Felix's "everything from scratch" rule and Loktar's animation check:

plaintext
Make a small 3D game that runs in the browser as one HTML file, using three.js.

The idea: [ONE SENTENCE: who the player is, what they do, how they win and how they lose].

Build everything yourself in code: models, textures, sound effects and music. No outside assets.

Characters use skeletal animation. Before you move on, render single frames of the walk and attack cycles and check them one by one until the motion looks right.

When it's playable, play it yourself: take screenshots at the start, the middle and the end, fix whatever looks wrong, then play again.

Stop when someone can play it start to finish. Tell me what you'd improve next, but don't do it yet.

Filled in, the idea line could look like this: "You're a lighthouse keeper on one stormy night. Climb the tower, refill the lamp and fix the shutters the wind keeps tearing open. You win if the lamp is still burning at dawn and lose if it goes dark for ten seconds." One sentence for who, what, win and lose is enough. The model fills in the rest, and the "how do I lose" part is the one people forget.

A video

Built on chetaslua's pure-code rule and Timur's "attach your own material" approach:

plaintext
Make a [30] second video about [YOUR STORY, PLACE OR PRODUCT].

I'm attaching [style images / a PDF with notes and photos]. Use them for the look and the facts. Don't copy any image directly.

Rules: every frame is drawn in code (JavaScript or Python), no stock footage, no outside assets except what I attached. Sound is made in code too.

First write a shot list: what we see, for how long, and what sound plays. Show it to me and wait.

After I say go: render one frame from each shot, look at them, fix what's off. Only then render the full video at 1080p, 60 fps, with sound.

Filled in, it could be: "Make a 30 second video about my grandmother's bakery in Lisbon. I'm attaching 6 photos of the shop and a note with the year it opened and the bread she's known for." That's the whole idea of Timur's Tuscany video: your real material, a short ask.

Once the short version works and you want more control over timing, this is the longer version I'd grow it into. It's the same idea, just with the decisions made upfront instead of left to the model:

plaintext
Make a 30 second video, 1920x1080, 60 fps, with sound.

STORY: [ONE SENTENCE: what happens, and what the viewer should feel at the end].

LOOK: [3 colors in hex, one font, one sentence about light and texture]. Use the attached images for style only.

SHOTS (seconds):
0-3    hook: [the single most striking image]
3-10   [what we learn]
10-20  [the turn or the surprise]
20-27  [the payoff]
27-30  a held final frame

MOTION: things ease in and out, nothing moves at a constant speed, one main movement per shot.

SOUND: made in code, no samples. Each sound lands one frame after the thing on screen that causes it. Music at about [100] BPM, cuts on the beat.

BUILD: one HTML file where every frame is a function of time, so any frame can be drawn on its own. Render the frames with Playwright, stitch them with ffmpeg.

CHECK: before the full render, show me one frame from each shot. After the render, look at a contact sheet of the whole video and fix anything that looks off.

A 3D walkthrough

Built on the floor plan method from everestchris6, plus a confirm-first step so it doesn't guess your measurements:

plaintext
Here is a floor plan [ATTACH] and photos of the space [ATTACH].

Build it as a 3D model to scale that I can walk through in the browser: walls, doors and windows exactly where the plan puts them, materials matched to the photos, soft daylight through the windows.

First, list every measurement you read from the plan and anything you're unsure about. Wait for me to confirm before you build.

When it's built, send me screenshots from three rooms at eye height. Compare them with the photos and fix anything that doesn't match before you call it done.

Filled in: attach the floor plan from a rental listing plus the listing photos, and you can tour the apartment before you go see it. The confirm-the-measurements step matters most here. Plans are often blurry, and a wall in the wrong place makes everything after it wrong.

A few things that will save you money on all three:

  • start in Claude Code or the Claude app with [top model] selected and effort on high
  • for a first try, ask for a 15 to 30 second video or a single level, then grow it
  • if a run is going to take hours, have [top model] write the plan and let [advanced model] do the long build
